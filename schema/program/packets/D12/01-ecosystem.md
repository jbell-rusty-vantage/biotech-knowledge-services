## 1. Composition authorities and reference databases

**Who.** USDA's FoodData Central (FDC) publishes several data types side by side: SR Legacy, Foundation, Survey (FNDDS) and branded records [S1–S7]. Phenol-Explorer publishes polyphenol contents of foods with processing effects [S14]. National composition tables (NCCDB from the University of Minnesota, USDA SR28, Canadian Nutrient File, Irish IFCDB, Dutch NEVO, UK CoFID, Australian NUTTAB) are the backbone that consumer apps license and merge [S24]. FoodOn is an OBO Foundry ontology for food products, organisms and parts, and processes [S25].

**Processes and operations.** Sampling and laboratory analysis of food samples, aggregation of samples into a published food record, calculated values for components that are not analysed, and periodic releases.
- FDC Foundation records list their analytical input samples (`inputFoods` of `dataType: Sample`) and carry per-nutrient `dataPoints`, `min`, `max`, `median` and `minYearAcquired` [S5]; the extra-virgin olive oil record reports saturated, monounsaturated and polyunsaturated fatty acids with 36 data points each [S4].
- SR Legacy is frozen: the McDonald's fries record is flagged `isHistoricalReference: true`, published 4/1/2019 [S1].
- Every nutrient value carries a derivation code: water, protein and fat in the McDonald's fries record are code `A` "Analytical" with 6 data points; energy is `NC` "Calculated" with 0 data points [S1].
- FNDDS (survey) foods are recipes over SR ingredients: "Cheeseburger (McDonalds)", food code 27510172, valid 1/1/2021–12/31/2023, has one input ingredient "Fast foods, cheeseburger; single, regular patty, with condiments" (SR code 21090) at 100 g, a WWEIA category "Burgers" (3702) and a "Moisture change: 0%" adjustment attribute [S6].
- Phenol-Explorer's oils report states content values and how processing changes them: in extra virgin olive oil 3,4-DHPEA-EDA, 3,4-DHPEA-EA, p-HPEA-EDA and p-HPEA-EA are 25, 7.2, 14 and 3.8 mg/100 g; in virgin olive oil (the report's grade for this statement, not extra virgin), frying at 180 °C reduces hydroxytyrosol and related secoiridoids by 40–50 % after 10 minutes and by about 90 % after six frying operations; microwave heating causes only minor losses; fermentation has a great impact on olive polyphenols [S14].

**What reference databases already do with brands and preparation.** FDC SR Legacy contains restaurant-branded items as reference foods: "McDONALD'S, french fries" (170721), "WENDY'S, french fries" (170726), "DENNY'S, french fries" (167672), "APPLEBEE'S, french fries" (169009); the McDonald's and Wendy's records sit in food category "Fast Foods", the Denny's and Applebee's records in "Restaurant Foods" [S7, S1]. The search also returns the FNDDS survey food "Cheeseburger (McDonalds)" (2706892) [S7]. Preparation is part of the food's identity string: "Potatoes, russet, flesh and skin, raw" (170027) and "Potatoes, Russet, flesh and skin, baked" (170030) are separate records [S2, S3]. Across those two records, water is 78.58 vs 74.45 g/100 g, potassium 417 vs 550 mg, vitamin C 5.7 vs 8.3 mg [S2, S3]; the fried, restaurant-branded record has 36.63 g water, 15.47 g fat and 189 mg sodium per 100 g [S1]. (Per-100 g values shift with water loss; a preparation comparison must state the basis.)

**Portions.** FDC portions are named sizes with gram weights, e.g. the McDonald's fries record lists small 71 g, medium 117 g, large 154 g and kids 31 g [S1].

**What they do not cover.** FDC carries no polyphenol fields for extra virgin olive oil (only fatty-acid classes among the fields we read) [S4] and no process contaminants such as acrylamide (none of the fries records list it) [S1, S7]; those live in Phenol-Explorer [S14] and FDA contaminant surveys [S11].

**Interview: FDC food record (5 instances read end to end: S1, S2, S3, S4, S5; plus FNDDS S6).** Fields: `fdcId`, `description` (identity-bearing text incl. preparation), `dataType` (SR Legacy | Foundation | Survey (FNDDS) | Branded), `foodClass` (FinalFood | Survey), `publicationDate`, `ndbNumber`, `foodCode` (FNDDS), `startDate`/`endDate` (FNDDS validity), `isHistoricalReference`, `foodCategory {id, code, description}` or `wweiaFoodCategory`, `scientificName` (some SR), `footnote` (Foundation), `foodAttributes[]` (typed attributes, e.g. moisture adjustment, WWEIA category), `foodComponents[]`, `nutrientConversionFactors[]`, `inputFoods[]` (Foundation: analytical samples; FNDDS: recipe ingredients with `ingredientCode`, `ingredientWeight`, `retentionCode`), `foodPortions[] {gramWeight, amount, modifier, measureUnit, portionDescription}`, `foodNutrients[] {nutrient {id, number, name, unitName, rank}, amount, dataPoints, min, max, median, minYearAcquired, foodNutrientDerivation {code, description, foodNutrientSource}, nutrientAnalysisDetails}`. Capturable: yes (public JSON API). Agent-ready: high (stable ids, typed numbers, derivation codes) but locators are JSON paths, not text spans; DEMO_KEY rate limits (tool log T3).

**Interview: phytochemical composition report (Phenol-Explorer oils; two sections read: olive/olive oil and sesame/sesame oil).** Fields are prose, not a schema: food and part, compound name, content value with unit and basis (mg/100 g), assay family (e.g. "Folin" for totals vs individual compounds), numbered reference ids, processing effects (frying, microwave, boiling, storage, fermentation, refining, bleaching) stated as percentage changes [S14]. Capturable: yes (HTTP page). Agent-ready: low-medium (numbers in sentences; the database's per-compound tables and retention factors are not exposed as an API in what we fetched).

## 2. Regulators, public-health agencies and hazard classifiers

**FDA (US).**
- *Menu labeling.* A covered establishment is a restaurant or similar retail food establishment that is part of a chain with 20 or more locations doing business under the same name; it must declare calories for each standard menu item "as usually prepared and offered for sale", rounded to 5-calorie increments up to 50 and 10-calorie increments above, must state "Additional nutrition information available upon request", and must have "a reasonable basis" for its nutrient declarations, which may come from nutrient databases, cookbooks, laboratory analyses or Nutrition Facts labels [S20]. The eCFR page we captured is "up to date as of 10/06/2026" [S20].
- *Process contaminants.* FDA's acrylamide page explains that acrylamide forms from sugars and asparagine "during high-temperature cooking, such as frying, roasting, and baking", publishes brand-level results from individual product samples through 2004 (and 2011/2015 data as downloads), and warns that because of unit-to-unit and lot-to-lot variation "data are not designed to be used to inform consumer food choices" [S11]. In the data through October 7, 2004, "McDonald's French Fries" are listed at 428 ppb and "Chick-fil-A Waffle Potato Fries" at 271 ppb; the limit of quantitation is 20 ppb (values below are reported as ND) and "Frozen products were not given additional cooking prior to testing" [S11]. The data through November 15, 2002 list seven McDonald's locations, 155–497 ppb (locations 1–7: 193, 328, 155, 326, 245, 270, 497), and Chick-fil-A french fries at 389 ppb [S11]. (Adversarial review: the research stage read only the first three McDonald's rows of the flattened table; counts re-checked against the origin HTML tables, 2026-10-09.)
- *Outbreak investigations and recalls.* The WanaBana cinnamon applesauce investigation page records: cinnamon sampled at the Austrofood facility in Ecuador at up to 5,110 ppm lead, "more than 2,000 times" the 2.5 ppm level Codex was considering for bark spices; a recalled pouch at 2.18 ppm lead versus the 0.01 ppm action level FDA proposed in draft guidance for foods for babies and young children; chromium at 1201 and 531 ppm in two cinnamon samples and 0.590 and 0.566 ppm in the recalled puree; FDA's leading hypothesis of "economically motivated adulteration"; warning letters to Austrofood (August 9, 2024) and Dollar Tree (June 2024); import alerts on the cinnamon distributor Negasmart; and CDC case counts appended as dated updates (e.g. "As of March 22 … a total of 519 cases") [S12]. openFDA's food enforcement API returns the corresponding recall records: recalling firm "WANABANA USA LLC", recall numbers F-0403-2024 and F-0404-2024 (Weis and Schnucks private-label pouches), Class I, voluntary, initiated 2023-10-29, terminated 2024-03-04, 2,998,088 units, reason_for_recall "North Carolina Department of Health and Human Services (NCDHHS) sampled product and found it to contain high levels of lead." (verbatim, re-fetched 2026-10-09; recall numbers F-0402/0403/0404-2024 carry the same text), with lot codes [S13].
- *Food–drug interactions.* FDA's consumer update explains that grapefruit juice can block intestinal CYP3A4 so that more of some drugs (some statins such as simvastatin and atorvastatin) stays in the body, while for fexofenadine grapefruit juice can reduce absorption by acting on drug transporters, and the fexofenadine label says "Do not take with fruit juices" [S21].

**EU.** Regulation (EU) No 432/2012 lists authorised health claims; for "Olive oil polyphenols" the authorised wording is that they "contribute to the protection of blood lipids from oxidative stress", with the condition that the claim may be used only for olive oil containing at least 5 mg of hydroxytyrosol and its derivatives per 20 g, and the consumer must be told the effect is obtained with a daily intake of 20 g [S16]. This is a composition threshold attached to a claim, i.e. a regulator's rule that makes "how much of compound X is in this lot of food Y" decisive.

**IARC (WHO).** IARC evaluated the consumption of red meat (Group 2A, probably carcinogenic) and processed meat (Group 1, carcinogenic to humans) in Monographs Volume 114, summarized in The Lancet Oncology [S17, S38]. Its Q&A states that every 50 g portion of processed meat eaten daily increases colorectal cancer risk by about 18 % (analysis of 10 studies), that Group 1 classification describes the strength of evidence and does **not** mean processed meat is as dangerous as tobacco, and that high-temperature cooking generates compounds that may contribute to carcinogenic risk, a role "not yet fully understood" [S18]. Hazard classes are about strength of evidence for a food group, not a dose-specific risk.

**Dietary guidance.** The Dietary Guidelines for Americans 2025–2030 (published at realfood.gov) frame advice around "real food" and "a dramatic reduction in highly processed foods", recommend fermented foods (sauerkraut, kimchi, kefir, miso) for the microbiome, keep saturated fat at no more than 10 % of daily calories, and say one meal should contain no more than 10 g of added sugars, pointing snack limits to FDA "Healthy" claim limits [S19]. The guideline uses "highly processed", not NOVA's "ultra-processed" [S19, S36] — two vocabularies for the same harm discourse.

**Interview: contaminant survey table (FDA acrylamide; two tables read: data through Nov 15, 2002 and through Oct 7, 2004; the page has four incremental tables — through 2002-11-15, 2003-02-07, 2003-10-01, 2004-10-07 — whose product rows almost never repeat, so a row's reporting window runs from the previous table's cutoff to its own).** Fields: food category (only on the first row of each group after flattening), product description including brand and sometimes location or sample number ("McDonald's french fries, location 1"; "Karamba Cafe French Fries, Sample 1"), acrylamide (ppb), ND/LOQ notes, analysing laboratory note, collection notes (restaurant/take-out vs frozen uncooked), table date [S11]. ## 3. Food companies: restaurants, packaged food, longevity-food brands, supply chains

The page names the potato varieties ("Russet Burbank and the Shepody") [S8]. It gives no serving weight in grams [S8]; FDC's historical record gives a medium serving of 117 g [S1].
- Derived observation (our arithmetic, not a source claim): FDC 170721's 323 kcal/100 g × 117 g ≈ 378 kcal for a medium serving, against 320 Cal on the current McDonald's page [S1, S8]. **Alleged longevity-food brands.** Blueprint (Bryan Johnson) sells an extra virgin olive oil marketed "for longevity with 400 mg/kg polyphenols", from olives "harvested in Portugal, during the harvest month of November 2025", with a current-harvest analysis of "425 mg/kg total biophenols (HPLC)", 80 % oleic acid, 3.7 meq/kg peroxide and 0.21 % free fatty acids, a usage instruction of 1–3 tablespoons daily, a Nutrition Facts panel (1 tbsp, 120 Calories, 14 g fat) and a statement that it is third-party tested for heavy metals [S15]. ## 4. Researchers and trials (science lens)

- *Ultra-processed diets, mechanistic RCT.* In a 20-person inpatient crossover trial at the NIH Clinical Center, ad libitum energy intake was 508 ± 106 kcal/day higher on an ultra-processed than an unprocessed diet; the diets were matched for presented calories, energy density, macronutrients, sugar, sodium and fiber; foods were "categorized according to the NOVA system"; meals were designed in ProNutra with values from USDA SR Release 26 and FNDDS 4.0; the authors state "Our study was not designed to identify the cause of the observed differences in energy intake" [S28]. The registry record (NCT03407053) registers both diets with intervention type `DIETARY_SUPPLEMENT` and primary outcome "Ad Libitum Energy Intake" over 14 days [S29].
- *UPF within dietary guidelines.* A 2 × 2 crossover feeding trial of 55 adults in England on two 8-week ad libitum diets that both followed the UK Eatwell Guide found weight loss on both, with greater percent weight change on the minimally processed diet (Δ%WC −1.01, 95 % CI −1.87 to −0.14) [S35].
- *Epidemiology of UPF.* An umbrella review of 45 pooled analyses graded convincing (class I) evidence for associations of greater UPF exposure with cardiovascular-disease mortality (RR 1.50; GRADE very low), type 2 diabetes (dose-response RR 1.12; moderate), anxiety and common mental disorder outcomes, and highly suggestive evidence for all-cause mortality (RR 1.21; low) and adverse sleep-related outcomes (OR 1.41; low); 22 pooled analyses were rated low and 19 very low quality under GRADE [S32]. The two grading systems (credibility class vs GRADE) disagree in the same row — an assessment must say which method.
- *NOVA as a method.* NOVA groups foods by the extent and purpose of industrial processing; Monteiro et al. give an operational rule: a product is ultra-processed if its ingredient list contains at least one item characteristic of the group (substances rarely used in kitchens such as hydrogenated oils or hydrolysed proteins, or cosmetic additive classes such as flavours, colours, emulsifiers, anti-foaming agents) [S36]. Hall et al. note that NOVA "has been criticized as being too imprecise and incomplete" while Brazil's dietary guidelines use it [S28]. Applied to the captured ingredient statements, the McDonald's fries list "Hydrogenated Soybean Oil" and a natural flavour from hydrolysed wheat and milk [S8], and the Wendy's UK frying oil lists an anti-foaming agent [S9] (classification itself would be a derived projection, not a stored fact).
- *Mediterranean diet, EVOO and nuts — and its retraction.* PREDIMED's 2013 NEJM report (PMID 23432189) is now a "Retracted Publication" with a "RetractionIn" link (PMID 29897867) and a "RepublishedIn" link to the 2018 article (PMID 29897866) [S30]. The 2018 version reports protocol deviations (household members enrolled without randomization, non-randomized assignment at one site) and revised estimates (HR 0.69 for EVOO and 0.72 for nuts) and changes the conclusion from "reduced the incidence of major cardiovascular events" to "the incidence … was lower among those assigned" [S30]. The trial is registered as ISRCTN35739639 [S30]; a ClinicalTrials.gov API v2 search on 2026-10-09 (`query.term=AREA[Acronym]PREDIMED` and `query.term=ISRCTN35739639`) returned no record, so it is not registered there (`_notes/tool-log.md` R3; the PubMed XML alone cannot support that absence).
- *Seed oils / linoleic acid.* Recovered data from the Minnesota Coronary Experiment showed lower serum cholesterol but no mortality benefit, a 22 % higher risk of death per 30 mg/dL reduction in serum cholesterol (HR 1.22), and a meta-analysis of five RCTs (n = 10,808) with no benefit on CHD or all-cause mortality from replacing saturated fat with linoleic acid [S33]. This is a disconfirmation source that both sides of the seed-oil controversy cite.
- *Fermented foods.* A 17-week randomized study (n = 18/arm) found the high-fermented-food diet steadily increased microbiota diversity and decreased inflammatory markers; the abstract reports the primary outcome (cytokine response score) as unchanged in the sentence describing the high-fiber diet [S31]. A small trial with a null primary outcome is exactly the shape an alleged-longevity-food claim must be checked against.
- *Time-restricted eating.* The TREAT trial (116 participants) concluded that TRE "in the absence of other interventions, is not more effective in weight loss than eating throughout the day" (between-group −0.26 kg, P = .63) and reported a between-group difference in appendicular lean mass index (−0.16 kg/m², P = .005) [S34]; a 2022 NEJM RCT compared calorie restriction with and without TRE [S37] (abstract not read in this stage).
- *Blue zones.* Newman's preprint reports that US supercentenarian status is predicted by the absence of vital registration, that introduction of birth certificates coincides with a 69–82 % fall in supercentenarian numbers, and that in Italy remarkable age is predicted by short average lifespan, raising "serious questions about the validity" of research on remarkable-age populations [S27]. This is the main disconfirmation artifact behind "blue zone foods".

## 5. Community, apps and discourse (community lens)

- *Diet-tracking apps* merge composition databases and user submissions: Cronometer curates user-submitted foods, recommends NCCDB (17,000+ foods, 70 nutrients) or USDA SR28 (8,000+ foods) for the most detailed data, and also loads CNF, IFCDB, NEVO, CoFID and NUTTAB plus its own compiled CFCD [S24]. The same food therefore exists as many records with different provenance; an app's number is a derived value from a chosen record.
- *Crowd databases* (OFF) expose revisions and their own quality warnings [S22, S23] — the community lens's strongest artifact is also the least trustworthy per value.
- *Longevity influencers as sellers.* Blueprint positions EVOO as "15% of Bryan's caloric intake" and as a longevity product [S15]; the recommendation and the sale come from the same organization (D7 financial-interest seam, D9 recommendation pattern).
- *Blue-zones discourse* is challenged by demographic data-quality evidence [S27], and the PREDIMED retraction [S30] shows that even the flagship Mediterranean-diet trial changed its causal wording.
- Not captured this stage: podcast and video rankings of foods (owned by D9; to be sampled in the fixture stage if a food fixture needs a recommendation occurrence).

## 6. Economics lens (investor)

- Regulation sets disclosure: chains with 20+ locations must disclose calories and provide written nutrition information on request [S20]; EU health claims for olive oil require a measured hydroxytyrosol threshold [S16]. Which companies can make which claims is a function of composition and jurisdiction.
- Policy direction: the 2025–2030 US guidelines call for a dramatic reduction of highly processed foods and point snack sugar limits to FDA "Healthy" claim limits [S19]; a UPF evidence base of trials [S28, S35] and associations [S32] underlies that shift.
- Harm events have named commercial actors and quantities: 2,998,088 units recalled, Class I, with warning letters to a manufacturer and a national retailer [S12, S13].
- Premium "longevity foods" carry prices and harvest-specific assays (Prolon $215.00 list, $182.75 sale [S26]; Blueprint harvest assay 425 mg/kg [S15]); price and listing belong to D6.
